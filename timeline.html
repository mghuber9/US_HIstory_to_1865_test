1325

Andes: Inca EmpireA large Andean empire links diverse peoples through political authority, roads, agriculture, and tribute.
Southwest: Pueblo communitiesIrrigation and settled village traditions remain central in the arid Southwest.
Eastern / Mississippi: Woodland & Mississippian societiesAgricultural towns and regional networks occupy much of eastern North America.
Plains & Northwest: Environment shapes cultureHunting cultures dominate much of the Plains, while rich coastal resources support Northwest communities.
Why it matters → When Europeans arrive, they encounter many established societies with different governments, economies, religions, and adaptations—not a single Native culture.

Renaissance Europe

Follow the European changes that encouraged exploration, then compare exploration with conquest and colonization in the Americas.

Date

1347–1351
Europe & Society: Black DeathEurope loses nearly half its population in Casey's notes. Labor shortages weaken feudalism and help merchants, craftspeople, and towns gain importance.
Why it matters → The social and economic changes after the plague help create the conditions for the Renaissance and stronger monarchies able to finance exploration.
mid-1300s–1600s
Europe & Society: RenaissanceA broad “rebirth” in art, learning, science, commerce, and political thought reshapes Western Europe.
Exploration: Age of Exploration growsImproved navigation, mapmaking, ships, and investment help England, France, Portugal, and Spain project power overseas.
1492–1494
Religion & Politics: Catholic powers divide claimsThe Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) attempts to divide newly claimed lands between Spain and Portugal.
Exploration: Columbus reaches the CaribbeanOn October 12, 1492, Columbus's voyage brings sustained European expansion into the Western Hemisphere.
Conquest & Colonization: Contact transforms Native lifeDisease, coercion, labor demands, and European settlement begin devastating many Native populations.
1497–1507
Exploration: Cabot & VespucciJohn Cabot reaches Newfoundland for England (1497). Amerigo Vespucci calls the lands a new continent (1499); “America” appears on a map in 1507.
1517–1555
Religion & Politics: Protestant ReformationMartin Luther challenges the Catholic Church; religious divisions reshape European politics. The Treaty of Augsburg (1555) recognizes rulers' power to choose religion in their territories.
1519–1531
Exploration: Magellan expeditionThe expedition begun by Ferdinand Magellan in 1519 demonstrates the global scale of oceanic exploration.
Conquest & Colonization: Spanish conquestCortés and Native allies defeat the Mexica capital; Pizarro attacks the Inca Empire in 1531. Spain expands systems of tribute, forced labor, missions, and conversion.
Why it matters → European colonization is driven by intertwined motives—wealth, power, and religion—and it radically alters Native societies.
1565
Religion & Politics: Catholic–Protestant rivalryEuropean religious conflict spills into colonial competition in North America.
Conquest & Colonization: St. AugustineSpain establishes St. Augustine in Florida, the first permanent European settlement in what becomes the United States.
1588
Europe & Society: England's naval position risesThe defeat of the Spanish Armada helps shift the balance of Atlantic power toward England.
Religion & Politics: England vs. SpainProtestant England and Catholic Spain clash amid privateering, empire, and religious rivalry.
1680
Conquest & Colonization: Pueblo RevoltPopé organizes a major revolt that drives Spanish colonists from New Mexico and restores Native control for a time.

Native Relations: Powhatan Confederacy nearbyAbout 30 Native chiefdoms under Powhatan surround the English settlement, making diplomacy, trade, and conflict central to survival.

1612–1616

Labor & Power: Demand for labor expandsTobacco profits make land and labor increasingly valuable, increasing reliance on indentured servants.
1610s–1700s
Labor & Power: Indentured servitudeMany migrants exchange years of labor for Atlantic passage. Freedom can bring opportunity, but many former servants remain poor or landless.
1676
Settlements: Jamestown burnedBacon's followers march on Jamestown and burn it during the rebellion.
Native Relations: Violence against Native peoplesNathaniel Bacon mobilizes men for attacks against Native communities after disputes on the Virginia frontier.
Labor & Power: Bacon's RebellionSocial tension among wealthy planters, poor settlers, and former servants erupts. Bacon challenges Governor William Berkeley before dying of fever.
Why it matters → The rebellion exposes deep class and frontier tensions in Virginia and helps frame later changes in the colony's labor system.

Early 1600s
Population & Family: Colonial survival is difficultEarly settlements suffer very high death rates, but abundant land and migration eventually contribute to rapid population growth.
Regional Economies: Different environments, different economiesNew England's rocky soil encourages mixed farming and maritime work; the Chesapeake and South increasingly favor staple crops.
Labor: Labor is scarceCheap land and expensive labor create strong demand for indentured workers and other labor systems.
17th century
Population & Family: Large colonial familiesEarlier marriage and high birth rates fuel natural population growth, even as childbirth and infant mortality remain severe.
Regional Economies: Cash crops expandTobacco becomes widespread in Virginia and Maryland; rice, sugarcane, and indigo become important farther south.
Labor: Indentured servitude remains importantContract labor supplies much of the workforce, especially before the rapid growth of racial slavery.
Race & Slavery: Slavery expandsColonies increasingly turn to enslaved African labor as plantation agriculture grows.
1660s
Labor: Labor systems divergeIndentured servitude continues, but permanent hereditary slavery grows in importance.
Race & Slavery: Slave codes hardenColonial laws increasingly define enslaved people as slaves for life, make status hereditary, and restrict movement, assembly, and legal rights.
Why it matters → Slavery is not simply a labor practice; colonial law increasingly builds a permanent race-based social system around it.
Late 1600s
Population & Family: Settled communities deepenTowns, farms, churches, and households become more established as the colonial population grows.
Regional Economies: Regional inequality growsSouthern landowners can build great wealth through plantation agriculture, while northern towns grow around farms, shipping, crafts, and trade.
Race & Slavery: Slavery legal across the coloniesBy the late 17th century, slavery is legalized throughout the English colonies.
Early 1700s
Regional Economies: Atlantic economy intensifiesSugar, rum, shipping, plantation crops, and transatlantic commerce bind colonial regions to the wider Atlantic economy.
Labor: Indentured labor declinesCasey's lecture describes a decline in indentured servitude alongside a rapid rise in enslaved African labor.
Race & Slavery: Slave trade expandsMore trading companies and plantation demand increase the forced migration and sale of enslaved Africans.
1739
Race & Slavery: Stono RebellionAn armed rebellion in South Carolina demonstrates active resistance to slavery and the violence built into the system.
1750
Labor: A transformed workforceColonial labor now includes free farmers and artisans, servants, wage workers, and a very large enslaved population.
Race & Slavery: Nearly 250,000 enslaved peopleCasey's notes estimate almost a quarter-million enslaved people living in the colonies by 1750.

Religion: Puritan educational goalsLiteracy is closely tied to scripture and religious life in New England.
Education & Print: Harvard CollegeMassachusetts Puritans found Harvard, beginning a long connection between denominations and colonial higher education.
c. 1685 onward
Ideas & Politics: Enlightenment / Age of ReasonScientific inquiry, evidence, natural rights, human progress, and political freedom increasingly challenge inherited authority.

Religion: Salem witch crisisAccusations beginning around Tituba grow into mass prosecutions. Nineteen people are ultimately hanged and Giles Corey is pressed to death.
Public Life: Community fear & authorityThe trials reveal the power of religious assumptions, social pressure, and local government in Puritan communities.

Education & Print: Yale & postal serviceYale is established by Connecticut Puritans in 1701; a colonial postal system begins operating in 1710.
Public Life: Taverns as communication hubsPublic houses serve as centers for news, mail, business, travel, and community exchange.
1730s–1750
Religion: First Great AwakeningItinerant preachers and revivalists emphasize personal conversion and weaken the control of established churches. The first wave is largely over by 1750.
Public Life: New religious networksBaptists, Methodists, and other groups gain influence, increasing religious diversity and competition.
Ideas & Politics: Individual judgment growsRevival religion and Enlightenment thought both encourage people to make judgments rather than simply accept inherited institutions.
Why it matters → The Enlightenment and Great Awakening arrive by different routes, but both help normalize questioning institutions and emphasizing individual belief or judgment.
1735–1745
Education & Print: Zenger & newspapersJohn Peter Zenger is acquitted in 1735 after criticizing New York's governor; by 1745 Casey notes 22 newspapers in the colonies.
Public Life: Public criticism spreadsPrint gives colonists more ways to circulate news, arguments, and criticism of officials.
Ideas & Politics: Early challenge to government authorityThe Zenger episode becomes an important example of resistance to official attempts to control criticism.
1746–1769
Religion: Denominations build institutionsPresbyterians, Anglicans, Baptists, Dutch Reformed Christians, and Congregationalists all sponsor colleges.
Education & Print: Colonial colleges multiplyPrinceton (1746), King's/Columbia (1754), Brown (1764), Rutgers (1766), and Dartmouth (1769) join earlier Harvard and Yale.
Ideas & Politics: More centers of learningHigher education helps spread competing religious, scientific, and political ideas across the colonies.

Colonial Conflicts

Compare British and French imperial expansion with Native alliances and the colonies' growing habit of self-government.

Date
British Empire
French Empire
Native Alliances
Colonial Self-Government
1608
French Empire: Quebec & New FranceSamuel de Champlain establishes Quebec and builds a commercial strategy around the fur trade and inland waterways.
Native Alliances: Trade creates alliancesFrench traders work closely with Native communities, creating partnerships that later shape imperial warfare.
1651–1663
British Empire: Navigation ActsParliament restricts colonial shipping and trade to keep wealth inside the British imperial system.
French Empire: New France tightenedIn 1663 Louis XIV reorganizes New France under a governor-general and sends more settlers and soldiers.
Colonial Self-Government: Trade laws breed resentmentColonists dislike imperial limits that conflict with local economic habits and elected assemblies.
1670s–1718
French Empire: France moves down the MississippiLa Salle reaches the Gulf in 1682 and claims Louisiana; New Orleans is established in 1718, strengthening French access to the interior.
Native Alliances: Interior networks matterControl of river routes and fur-trade relationships gives Native nations major strategic importance in imperial competition.
1684–1691
British Empire: Dominion of New EnglandJames II consolidates colonies and sends Edmund Andros to enforce royal authority, taxes, Anglicanism, and trade laws.
Colonial Self-Government: Colonists resistAfter the Glorious Revolution, colonists arrest Andros in 1689. John Locke's Two Treatises (1690) argues for natural rights and resistance to corrupt rulers.
Why it matters → Long before independence, colonists are already defending local political habits against stronger imperial control.
1721–1742
British Empire: Salutary neglectPrime Minister Robert Walpole allows colonial trade to operate with relatively loose enforcement.
Colonial Self-Government: Assemblies gain confidenceElected colonial legislatures control budgets and make self-government feel increasingly normal—first a habit, then a claimed right.
1754
British Empire: Washington & Fort NecessityVirginia sends 22-year-old George Washington into the Ohio Valley; his force is defeated at Great Meadows.
French Empire: Fort Duquesne & Ohio ValleyFrench control of strategic river territory threatens British westward expansion.
Native Alliances: Native allies are essentialBoth empires depend on Native alliances, knowledge, and military support.
Colonial Self-Government: Albany CongressBenjamin Franklin urges greater colonial unity against France, but the Albany Plan is rejected.
Why it matters → The French and Indian War begins in the colonies and forces colonists to think about cooperation across colonial boundaries.
1756–1759
British Empire: Britain expands the warThe colonial conflict becomes part of the global Seven Years' War; British forces capture key French positions, including Quebec in 1759.
French Empire: New France loses groundFrench forts and settlements come under increasing British pressure.
Native Alliances: Frontier warfare intensifiesNative nations fight on different sides according to their own interests, rivalries, and relationships with European powers.
1763
British Empire: Treaty of ParisBritain wins a vast North American empire—but also inherits the cost of defending it after years of expensive warfare.
French Empire: French territorial power collapsesFrance gives up most of its North American claims to Britain and Spain.
Native Alliances: Balance of power changesNative nations lose France as a major counterweight to British expansion.
Colonial Self-Government: Victory creates a new problemColonists expect greater opportunity after the war; British leaders instead look for ways to control territory and make the colonies help pay imperial costs.
Why it matters → The war that removes France as Britain's major North American rival also creates the debt, troops, and imperial policy changes that drive the next section.

Section 4.2

The Road to Revolution

The pace now tightens: British policies, colonial resistance, violence, and political ideas escalate from imperial reform to independence.

Date
British Actions
Colonial Resistance
Armed Conflict
Ideas & Unity
1763
British Actions: Proclamation LineBritain restricts settlement west of the Appalachian crest while trying to control the enormous territory won from France.
Colonial Resistance: Colonial expectations collide with policyMany colonists had expected access to western lands after helping fight the French and Indian War.
1764
British Actions: Sugar & Currency ActsGeorge Grenville tightens enforcement, changes revenue policy, and restricts colonial currency while Britain seeks money after the war.
Colonial Resistance: Imperial control feels differentColonists increasingly see postwar enforcement as an intrusion into practices developed during salutary neglect.
1765
British Actions: Stamp ActParliament imposes a direct tax on printed materials and legal documents.
Colonial Resistance: Boycotts & protestSons and Daughters of Liberty, crowds, merchants, and local activists pressure stamp officials and resist the tax.
Ideas & Unity: Stamp Act CongressColonies coordinate a formal statement of rights and grievances against parliamentary taxation.
1766
British Actions: Repeal + Declaratory ActParliament repeals the Stamp Act but simultaneously asserts its authority to legislate for the colonies.
Colonial Resistance: Resistance appears successfulBoycotts and pressure help produce repeal, encouraging colonists to use organized resistance again.
Ideas & Unity: The constitutional dispute remainsThe immediate tax disappears, but the argument over Parliament's authority is unresolved.
Why it matters → Repeal reduces one crisis without settling the central question: who has the right to govern and tax the colonies?
1767
British Actions: Townshend ActsNew duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea raise revenue and help pay royal governors.
Colonial Resistance: Nonimportation resumesColonists renew boycotts and resistance to taxation and stronger royal control.
Jan.–Apr. 1770
British Actions: Troops remain a flashpointBritish soldiers confront protestors in New York and Boston. Parliament later repeals most Townshend duties but keeps the tea tax.
Colonial Resistance: Liberty protestsThe Battle at Golden Hill begins over Liberty Poles; tensions remain high in Boston.
Armed Conflict: Boston Massacre — MarchBritish soldiers fire into a crowd, killing five and wounding eight. Crispus Attucks is among those killed.
Ideas & Unity: A powerful symbolThe killings become a lasting example used to portray standing troops and imperial power as threats to colonial liberty.
1772
Colonial Resistance: Gaspée IncidentRhode Island colonists board and burn the grounded HMS Gaspée.
Ideas & Unity: Committees of CorrespondenceSamuel Adams helps organize networks to circulate statements of rights and grievances.
1773
British Actions: Tea ActParliament gives the East India Company special access to the colonial tea market while the tea tax remains.
Colonial Resistance: Boston Tea Party — Dec. 16Patriots dump 46 tons of tea into Boston Harbor rather than accept the policy.
Ideas & Unity: Resistance becomes more dramaticThe destruction of tea turns a dispute over taxation into a major confrontation over imperial authority.
1774
British Actions: Coercive / Intolerable ActsBritain closes Boston's port, restructures Massachusetts government, expands quartering, and tightens royal control.
Colonial Resistance: Local resistance spreadsColonists take control of local governments, stockpile weapons and gunpowder, and organize economic resistance.
Ideas & Unity: First Continental Congress — Sept.Delegates coordinate a Declaration of Rights and the Continental Association, creating unprecedented intercolonial cooperation.
Why it matters → Britain's punishment of Massachusetts helps turn a local crisis into a continental one.
April 18–19, 1775
British Actions: March on Lexington & ConcordBritish troops move to capture rebel leaders and seize military supplies.
Colonial Resistance: Revere & Dawes warn the countrysideMilitia and minutemen mobilize as word of the British march spreads.
Armed Conflict: Lexington & ConcordShots are fired at Lexington; colonists later ambush British troops retreating from Concord.
Ideas & Unity: Political resistance becomes warAfter years of protest and coercion, organized armed conflict is now underway.
Why it matters → The question is no longer only whether Parliament can tax the colonies; colonists and British soldiers are now killing one another.
June–July 1775
British Actions: Britain presses the military responseBritish forces fight to hold Boston and reject colonial demands to end hostilities.
Colonial Resistance: Continental ArmyGeorge Washington is chosen to command a Continental Army on June 15.
Armed Conflict: Bunker Hill — June 17The British take the position but suffer more than twice the colonial casualties.
Ideas & Unity: Olive Branch PetitionIn July, colonists still ask George III to stop the fighting, showing that many are not yet committed to independence.
January 1776
Ideas & Unity: Common SenseThomas Paine argues that independence is both logical and inevitable, pushing public debate beyond reconciliation.
July 2–4, 1776
Colonial Resistance: Colonies become independent statesThe Continental Congress approves the independence resolution on July 2.
Armed Conflict: War continuesIndependence is declared while the military struggle with Britain is still underway.
Ideas & Unity: Declaration of Independence — July 4Congress adopts Jefferson's Declaration, edited by Franklin and John Adams, grounding independence in equality, unalienable rights, consent, and the right to alter or abolish abusive government.
Why it matters → The chain from postwar imperial reform to protest, coordinated resistance, violence, and political argument ends with a formal claim to independence.
